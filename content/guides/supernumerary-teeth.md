A parent in Kuala Lumpur might notice a small tooth appearing behind a child's front teeth. An adult may spot an unusual tooth in a mirror after years of assuming every tooth was in the usual position. These situations deserve a dental assessment, but an online description or photograph cannot confirm what the tooth is.

Supernumerary teeth are extra teeth that develop in addition to the usual number. A registered dentist can examine the mouth and decide whether further assessment is appropriate.

## What are supernumerary teeth?

Supernumerary teeth are teeth that form beyond the usual set of primary or permanent teeth. An extra tooth can appear in different parts of the mouth. For example, an additional tooth may sit near the upper front teeth, while another may remain below the gum and never become visible.

The tooth may look similar to a nearby tooth, or it may have a different shape and size. A visible gap, crowding, delayed eruption, or an unusual tooth position can prompt someone to seek advice. These signs alone do not identify supernumerary teeth.

A dentist may assess the tooth's position and relationship with nearby teeth using a clinical examination and, where appropriate, dental imaging. The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry includes supernumerary teeth among conditions that can require dental assessment in children. A registered dentist must interpret findings for an individual patient. If a clinic suggests a three-dimensional scan, [CBCT dental scans: questions to ask a listed clinic](/guides/cbct-dental-scan-questions) is a list of questions about need, records, and the fee. It does not say that an extra tooth requires that scan.

An extra tooth can be visible in the mouth or remain below the gum, so appearance alone cannot confirm supernumerary teeth.

### When not to rely on general information

General guides do not replace an examination when a tooth causes pain, bleeding, swelling, difficulty eating, an injury, or concern about delayed eruption. They also cannot tell you whether a tooth belongs to the primary or permanent set. Arrange an appointment with a registered dentist rather than attempting to identify or manage the tooth yourself. If the problem should not wait for a routine slot, the [urgent visits guide](/guides/dental-clinic-open-now-common-questions-about-urgent-visits) explains what to ask a clinic that can see you sooner.

## How supernumerary teeth relate to primary teeth and permanent teeth

Understanding the normal dental set helps explain why an extra tooth can be confusing. Primary teeth are the first set of teeth in childhood. Permanent teeth develop as the mouth changes and usually replace the primary teeth over time. The two sets have different names, shapes, and expected stages of eruption.

Supernumerary teeth can relate to either set. An additional tooth may appear alongside primary teeth, permanent teeth, or remain within the jaw. A child with a tooth appearing behind another tooth may have several possible explanations, and a parent cannot determine the cause from position alone.

For example, a child in Pahang may lose a primary tooth while another tooth appears in the same area. That observation does not prove the new tooth is supernumerary. The dentist will consider the child's dental history, the tooth's appearance, and its position before explaining what the finding means.

Bring earlier dental records or details about when teeth appeared and were lost. A short timeline can help the dentist understand the change without turning a parent's observation into a diagnosis.

## Using a teeth eruption chart as a general guide

A teeth eruption chart shows the usual sequence and approximate age ranges associated with primary and permanent teeth. It can help parents understand why a child's tooth development is being discussed, but it cannot identify supernumerary teeth.

Charts describe typical patterns. Individual children can develop on different schedules, and a chart cannot show the exact position of an unseen tooth. It also cannot distinguish every unusual tooth shape or confirm whether a visible tooth belongs to the normal set.

Use a teeth eruption chart as a list of questions for a dental visit. Note the child's age, which teeth have appeared or fallen out, and whether the concern affects eating or comfort. A dentist should assess concerns about tooth development, eruption, or an apparent extra tooth.

| What a teeth eruption chart can show | What it cannot confirm |
| --- | --- |
| Typical sequence of primary and permanent teeth | Whether a specific tooth is supernumerary |
| Approximate development periods | The position of a tooth below the gum |
| A useful starting point for questions | A diagnosis for an individual mouth |

A teeth eruption chart supports preparation for a dental visit. It does not replace a registered dentist's assessment.

## What questions can you ask a dentist?

A clear list of questions helps you leave the appointment with an accurate record of what the dentist observed. Ask:

- Does the tooth need an in-person assessment or follow-up?
- How does the dentist identify whether it is a primary tooth, permanent tooth, or possible supernumerary tooth?
- Is dental imaging appropriate for understanding the tooth's position?
- Could the tooth affect nearby teeth or eruption, based on the examination?
- What signs should prompt an earlier review?

Bring the date you first noticed the tooth, any symptoms, previous dental notes, and a list of relevant questions. A photo can show how the tooth looked on a particular day, but it does not replace an examination.

Ask the clinic to explain the next step in plain language. Do not assume that every extra-looking tooth needs the same response. The dentist's recommendation depends on the individual examination and available clinical information.
