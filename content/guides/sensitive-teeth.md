Sensitive teeth usually means a short, sharp twinge when you drink something cold, eat something sweet, or brush along the gum. It is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Several different problems feel similar, and a registered dentist has to sort them out. This page lists common causes in general terms, the questions worth asking, and the situations that should not wait for a convenient week.

## What people mean by sensitive teeth

MouthHealthy, from the American Dental Association, describes tooth sensitivity as pain from cold, heat, or sweet food when the inside of the tooth is no longer well covered. Enamel protects the crown, the part you see. A layer called cementum protects the root. Under both is dentine, which is less dense and contains tiny tubes. When enamel or cementum is lost, or when the gum pulls back, those tubes can let cold, heat, and acidic or sticky food reach the nerve.

The feeling is often brief. It can affect one tooth or many. A single tooth that hurts on biting is a different clue from a general wince with ice water. Tell the dentist which tooth, what sets it off, and how long the pain lasts. If a cavity is the question you are already asking, read [Caries dental care: questions to ask before choosing a clinic](/guides/dental-caries-questions) as well.

## Worn enamel

Enamel can thin. MouthHealthy includes worn tooth enamel among the causes of sensitive teeth. Acid from frequent fizzy drinks, citrus, sour sweets, or reflux can soften enamel. Hard brushing and a gritty powder can abrade it over time. Grinding or clenching can wear the biting edges. The dentist is the person who can say whether any of that is happening in your mouth.

Ask what they can see: a smooth cupped surface, a notch at the gum line, or a flattened biting edge. Ask whether they want you to change a drink, a brushing method, or a night guard. MouthHealthy warns that overuse of whiteners can damage enamel or gums.

## Gum recession and an exposed root

Gums can sit lower on the tooth than they used to, which exposes the root. MouthHealthy notes that dentine may be exposed when gums recede, and that gum disease is one possible cause of sensitivity. Brushing across the gum with a hard brush is another reason clinicians ask about your technique. Recession is not always painful. When the root is bare, cold air and cold water are common triggers.

A dentist checks how much root is showing, whether the gum is inflamed, and whether plaque or tartar is sitting on it. Treatment depends on that finding. MouthHealthy says a dentist may suggest a fluoride application, a filling or bonding to cover a worn area, or a gum graft if tissue has been lost from the root. Those are examples of options a clinician might discuss. They are not a menu you can choose from a webpage, and none of them is guaranteed to stop the feeling.

Tell the clinic if your teeth look longer than before, if food packs between them, or if the gums bleed. Bleeding needs an examination. Clinics that list routine care are under [General Dentistry](/services/general-dentistry). If the dentist thinks the gums need specialist care, ask what the referral is for and what happens while you wait.

## Decay, cracks, and worn fillings

MouthHealthy also lists tooth decay, fractured teeth, and worn fillings as causes. A cavity can let stimuli reach the inside of the tooth. A crack can hurt when you release a bite. An old filling can leak at the edge. These problems may affect one tooth, which is a useful detail to report.

The examination may include looking, a gentle test, and an X-ray if the dentist decides imaging would help. Ask what the image is meant to answer. Ask whether the tooth can be watched, whether it needs a filling, or whether the nerve may be involved. Pain relief from a pharmacy can take the edge off while you arrange a visit. It does not remove decay or close a crack.

If pain is severe and persistent and other care will not settle it, MouthHealthy says a dentist may discuss root canal treatment. That is a clinical decision after an examination, not a conclusion you should reach because a tooth dislikes ice. Ask what was found, what the alternatives are, and what the written estimate includes.

## Recent whitening

Whitening is a frequent reason people search for sensitive teeth. MouthHealthy says some people who use tooth whiteners get sensitivity because peroxide in the product passes through enamel to the dentine and irritates the tooth. It says this sensitivity is often temporary, and that you can pause and try again later. It also says overuse can damage enamel or gums, so the directions matter, and a dentist should be involved.

Sensitivity after an in-clinic whitening visit, a take-home tray, or a shop-bought strip is a reason to contact the clinic that provided it or your own dentist. Do not add a second product on top. Ask how long they expect the feeling to last, what should prompt a review, and whether your enamel and gums were suitable for whitening in the first place. Whitening does not treat decay or a receding gum. If the tooth was already sensitive, ask for an examination before any bleaching.

Clinics that list this service are grouped under [Teeth Whitening](/services/teeth-whitening). A listing means the clinic offers the service. It does not mean whitening is appropriate for you, and it does not publish a result you can expect.

## What to ask a dentist

Take a short history with you: when the feeling started, which teeth, what triggers it, and any recent whitening, new toothpaste, grinding, or dental work. Then ask:

1. Which tooth or teeth are involved, and what do you think is exposing them?
2. Do I have decay, a crack, a worn filling, gum recession, or enamel wear?
3. Do you need an X-ray or a gum check to answer that?
4. What can I change in brushing, diet, or clenching while we watch this?
5. If you suggest a desensitising toothpaste, a fluoride application, a filling, or gum care, what is that option trying to do, and what will you do if it does not help?
6. What is the fee for the examination, and for any treatment you recommend today?

MouthHealthy describes desensitising toothpaste as a product that needs several applications before sensitivity reduces, and fluoride gel as an in-clinic option that strengthens enamel. Those descriptions come from the association’s patient page. Your dentist decides whether either one fits the cause they found. A toothpaste that helps a neighbour can be the wrong step if your pain is a cavity.

If you do not understand the finding, ask the dentist to repeat it in plain language before you agree to treatment.

## When to book promptly

Arrange a dental visit soon if sensitive teeth is new, getting worse, or limited to one tooth. Book promptly, and tell the clinic it is painful, if:

- the pain is strong, lasts after the cold drink is gone, or wakes you at night;
- you cannot chew on one side;
- a tooth has chipped, cracked, or been knocked;
- the gum beside the tooth is swollen, or you have a fever or a bad taste;
- whitening pain is severe, or the gum has turned white or sore after a product.

Call the clinic before you travel. Listing hours change, and a directory cannot reserve a chair. The guide to [urgent dental visits](/guides/dental-clinic-open-now-common-questions-about-urgent-visits) explains how to read “open now” and when a hospital emergency department or a 999 call is the right step. Difficulty breathing or swallowing is an emergency medical problem, not a toothpaste problem.
